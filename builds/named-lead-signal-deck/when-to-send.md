# When to send it, and when not to

A signal deck is proof, not an opener. Get the timing wrong and it either burns hours on a
lead that was never going to buy, or it lands on someone who isn't ready to see it yet.

## What it's for

The deck's only job is to make a stalled conversation feel worth continuing. It works because
it shows instead of tells: instead of saying "we know your market," you hand over six real
companies in their exact world, each with a live reason to care, each with an opener already
written. That's harder to wave off than another paragraph of copy.

It is not a proposal. It does not explain your full offer, and it never states a price. It
proves you can do the work, then it gets out of the way.

## When to build one

Check every box before you start. If any of these is a no, don't build yet.

1. **You already sent a plain reply, and it didn't move the thread.** Most leads that stall
   don't need a deck, they need a better plain reply first. If you haven't tried that yet, try
   it before you spend hours on a deck.
2. **The lead is worth the time.** Could this company actually buy what you sell, at the size
   and budget you sell it at? Building a deck for someone who was never going to be a client is
   time you don't get back.
3. **You can name a real signal for their market.** Not a guess, a real one that passes the
   public, dated, causal test in `deck-template.md`. If you've genuinely tried and can't find
   one, stop. A deck without a real signal is a list of names with nothing behind them, and
   that reads worse than no deck at all.
4. **The lead is engaged enough to look at it.** They replied, they asked a question, they
   pushed back with a reason. Someone who never opened your first message won't open a deck
   either.

## When not to build one

- **Before you've qualified the lead at all.** Don't build proof for someone you haven't
  confirmed is a real fit yet. Qualify on the call or in the reply first, build second.
- **As a cold first-touch attachment.** Never send this on the first message to someone who's
  never heard from you. It's a mid-conversation move, not an icebreaker. Sending it cold wastes
  the strongest asset you have on someone who hasn't earned the effort yet, and hasn't shown
  they'll read it.
- **When a real signal doesn't exist.** Don't stretch a weak fact into a signal just to fill
  the deck. Six real rows beat eight with two invented ones, and a reader who checks one name
  and finds it thin will assume the rest are too.
- **When you're doing it instead of the harder fix.** If your reply rate is low because the
  offer itself isn't landing, or your first message doesn't say anything specific, a signal
  deck won't fix that. It's proof for a conversation that's already started, not a patch for
  outreach that never gets a reply in the first place.

## How to send it

Keep the deck and the ask for a call in two separate places.

- **The deck itself never asks for a call.** No calendar link, no "worth 15 minutes?", nothing
  that turns the last page into a pitch. End it on a plain statement, or nothing at all. The
  deck's job is to earn the next message, not to close it.
- **The message that carries the deck link is where you ask.** That message is short: one line
  on why you built this for them, the link, and two real times you're free. Something like,
  "Put this together from just your site and your reply. Free Thursday at 2 or Friday at 11 if
  either works." Keep it under 100 words.
- **If they asked you specific questions before you sent the deck, answer those questions in
  the message too**, in plain sentences, before the deck link. Skipping a question they
  directly asked reads as a dodge, and it will cost you the thread faster than a slow reply
  would have.

## The one thing worth repeating

If you can't find a real signal, don't build the deck. A decorative list of company names with
nothing checkable behind them is worse than sending nothing, because the first name a prospect
checks and finds hollow costs you the other seven.
